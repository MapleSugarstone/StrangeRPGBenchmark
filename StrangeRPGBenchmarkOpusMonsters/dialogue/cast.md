# WHORL cast sheet

## How to use this file

Find the speaker's register first, then their entry. The register decides how ordinary or strange a line sounds, and the entry says who the speaker is. Casual speakers have no habit at all. Formal and weird speakers each have one named habit. Use it in about one line out of three, at most once in a line, and never in two of that speaker's lines in a row. Write their other lines freely in their register, and reach for the other patterns in `dialogue/style.md` when a line invites one. Leave a line plain when plain is better. Variety is the point: the user's examples surprise because no two lines are built the same way. Never tack a short tail sentence onto an old line ("It sings. Flat, mostly."). That shape is used up. Rewrite the line from what the speaker wants or feels in the moment.

Never swap an in-world word from section 2 to serve a habit. The facts stay and the strangeness goes around them. A direction, price, number, hint, or rule must still be clear. Keep every term in section 2 and every {placeholder}. Keep every digit as well: the importer rejects a line whose digits change, so a line can move a digit but never add or remove one. Write any new number as a word. Stay under 100 characters. Use no em dash and no semicolon. Never copy a name or line from `Writing Examples.txt`.

A speaker marked (//) may split a long line across text boxes with ` // ` between parts, each part under 100 characters. No other speaker may. Speakers marked (earnest) mean every word they say. Odd things are stated flat and never explained. A row that is a cut-off stem or suffix stays unchanged.

## Registers

Every speaker belongs to one of three registers, described in `dialogue/style.md` under "Three registers come first". A scene works by contrast. Put a formal or weird line next to a flat casual answer, and let casual speakers underplay big moments.

**Casual** is the default, and any speaker not named below is casual. Casual speakers talk like ordinary people: short, loose, contractions, "uh", "yeah", "kinda", "I think", a one-word reaction, a sentence that trails off. The softest or saddest may end a line on :-] or :-( now and then. They have no habit and nothing strange of their own. What they talk about can still be odd (a house that turned, a cast on the porch), but they say it as if it were nothing.

- Main cast: Ouro's choices, Small Gran, Tack, Bare, and Cinch (the real one).
- Keepers and guides: Knuckle, the Ringer, Turnwise, the Mudlark, and the Man at the well.
- Hermits: every Hermit, Tellin, and Murex.
- Everyone else: townsfolk, children, trainers, gym helpers, guards, the Shellwright, the Conjoiner, the Counter, the Ring polisher, the Label-reader, the Ice-fisher, the Catcher, the Pruner, the Listener, the Lunch-bringer, the Tooth-sitter, the letters, and every speaker with only a few lines.

**Formal** speakers think they are important and reach too far. They use grand words, invented formal words that are a little wrong ("most expectational", "a hermitous disturbance"), and stacked adjectives. They may open on a greeting in capitals, and they put two or three exclamation marks on a line when they get excited. They are sincere about all of it. Invent new words for each speaker and do not repeat these samples.

- Gran, Leeward, the Verger, Grafton, Old Amber, Quillon, Fid, Purchase, the Sifter, Gloss, Siphon, the Tide-reader, the Point-watcher, the Master, the Upright Man, the Stackkeeper, and the Reeve.
- Any clerk, Boardmaster, Hall keeper, or Cartographer.
- Bezel, the gem setter.

**Weird** speakers carry the strangeness with a quirk from `dialogue/style.md`. Keep this register to the speakers listed here.

- The Strandmonger and his Register entries, Cinch's cast (the Holdfast, Knot), the Gleaner, Mundane and the other whorl rows, Ohm, Lug, Hasp, Perihel, the bottle girl, the Eldest, and Druse.

Narration, look, choice prompt, and goal are plain. Their entries are at the end.

## 1. The world in five sentences

Everything that grows leaves its old shell behind, and the empty shell gets up and walks as a creature called a whorl. People turn once, at no set age, when their first shell (their cast) comes off and becomes a companion, and a child who has not turned is a late one. The world is the Volute, a great shell lying open to the sky and held shut by eight giant Stays driven through its edge (the Lip), and a Staykeeper tends each Stay and gives a pearl. Ouro, late, walks the Lip while the Hermits under Bare pull the Stays out, and each Stay that comes loose cracks Ouro's own shell. Past the Margin lies the Strand, a night beach where whole worlds lie like seashells and where the Gleaner, a giant child's cast, carries off every shell with nobody moving in it.

## 2. Words to keep exactly

Never change these. Never write the old words slough, peg, tan, rind, Drum, Rim, Crown, Key, Peel, or scale.

- whorl: a shell that walks. cast: the shell a person leaves when they turn. late one: a person who has not turned.
- turn, Turning Day, second turning. aperture: the clear spot where a whorl's owner left. craze: a fine crack in the ground.
- horn, sound (the verb): the device that catches a whorl, and the act. Periwinkle, whelk, triton, nautilus horns run weakest to strongest.
- your four: the whorls you walk with. out and in reserve. the Midden: where spare whorls wait.
- conjoining, the Conjoiner: joining two whorls for good. notion: a small held item. nacre: layers a Shellwright adds to a stat.
- tide: the battle resource. wearing: climbing into a whorl you keep to cross ground. Prise, Shellboard: minigames.
- pearl: a Staykeeper's badge. cowries: money. the Register: the Strandmonger's book of every kind. grotto, Shellwright: the shop building and its keeper.
- the Volute, the Lip, the Apex, the Operculum, the Holdfast, the Stays, a Staykeeper, the Hands (Fid, Lug, Hasp, Purchase), the Climb.
- the Hermits, the Hermitage, the Exuvia, the Riders, the Shallows, the Margin, the Stack, the Knucklebones, the Moonwater, the Dry Sea, the Midden Road.
- Towns: Turnstone, Rib, Mast, Spire, Bole, Hum (the Pylon), Tusk, Hilt, Fall. Whorl names such as Tackle, Brack, Knot, Full, Mundane, Welling.
- Strand words: the Strand, low and high water, spring low and spring high, the sweep (everything empty is taken at the end of a low), tide bells, the hand bell, the tide table, star grains, Starfall, Strand level.
- the gray: a standing wall of water. the blank: unfinished pale ground. labels: the Gleaner's driftwood capitals. the Tray, the Conch.
- Strand places: the Outer Whorl, the Wrack, the Flats, the Sand Dollar, the Cowrie, the Polish, the Auger, the Nautilus, the Low Line, the Long Strand.

## 3. The cast

### Main cast

**Ouro and choice** (95, casual, earnest). Speaks only through the player's choices, four words or fewer. Choices are menu options, so they stay plain words the player reads at a glance, said the way a young person would say them. Leave most of them as they are. A refusal to answer can be only "...".
"Say again" can become "Say that again?".

**Gran** (34, formal, earnest). Very old and sharp. She tells Small Gran what to tell "my little turnip" and never speaks to Ouro. Other characters avoid calling Ouro a child. Her formality is old and exact rather than loud: full words, a grand or old-fashioned word now and then, and never an exclamation mark.
Habit: after "Tell my little turnip" she drops "is", "'s", or "there's", the way an old formula would.
"Tell my little turnip the road's dry this time of year." becomes "Tell my little turnip the road dry this time of year." "Tell my little turnip there's soup." becomes "Tell my little turnip soup."
Two rows stay plain: "You gave her to a child." and a row that is only "Tell my little turnip".

**Small Gran** (6, casual). Do not rewrite. Fixed lines: "Is it today?", "Mum says don't scratch.", "I can feel it starting.", "Don't look. Don't look yet.", "There. There it goes.", and "There. Now you."

**Tack** (74, casual). Ouro's rival, who wants to hold the world still. He talks like a kid keeping score: short, flat, a bit smug. Nearly every line has a number because he counts everything. Move a digit to the front of a sentence when it lands better there, and let the last sentence shrink a big claim to a small count. Big moments stay small in his mouth.
"Cinch has stood up there 40 years. 0 days off. I can stand still 1 morning." can become "40 years Cinch has stood up there. 0 days off. 1 morning I can do, if there's lunch."
"1. That's 1." can become "1. Yeah. That's 1."

**The Strandmonger** (43, weird). Old trader in Turnstone who keeps the Register. He prices everything, including things with no price, in cowries and in stranger currency (shoes, half a sandwich, the birds). He never doubts a price.
Habit, tic in the wrong place: a line starts with "And", "Which", or "Plus", as if adding to a bill already running.
"Good. That one cost me two cowries and a sandwich." becomes "And good. That one cost me two cowries and a sandwich."

**Bare** (40, casual, earnest). Leader of the Hermits, who never turned. Calm, kind, and short. Most of her lines are instructions to her Hermits, said the way you would ask someone to pass the salt. She never shouts and never explains.
"Walk away from the Mast." can become "Okay. Walk away from the Mast." Her one break stays plain: "I wanted you to have it."

**Cinch, the Holdfast** (//, weird) (also "Cinch's cast" and Knot). A cast at the Operculum. Voice: only obligations (must, has to, needs, will not). Lines with "has to" are this Cinch.
Habit, loud: all capitals, one long run-on piled with AND, no final stop, often cut off by a hyphen.
"The Volute has to hold." becomes "THE VOLUTE HAS TO HOLD AND SOMEONE HAS TO STAND HERE WHILE IT HOLDS AND-"
"You will have to make me take my hands off it." becomes "YOU WILL HAVE TO MAKE ME TAKE MY HANDS OFF IT AND I WILL NOT // TAKE THEM OFF AND YOU WILL HAVE TO MAKE ME AND-"

**Cinch, the real one** (91, casual, earnest). Old, in a pale robe, forty years out on the Strand. Easy and unhurried. Most of his lines invite someone to sit or say what a sitting person notices. He drops "I" and "it" at the start of a sentence the way relaxed people do.
"Sit down. I saw it come over from the fire." can become "Sit down. Saw it come over from the fire."

**The Gleaner** (weird). A giant child's cast. It never speaks, and its capital-letter labels are not in the files.
Habit, silence: narration about it names a body part as the subject, rarely says "it", and ends on "..." where it does not answer.
"It is curled, with its knees up and its back to the beach." becomes "Knees up. Back to the beach. Curled round nothing. ..."

### Staykeepers

**Knuckle** (9, casual). Rib keeper, fists bigger than the body. A big, friendly fighter who says little and means it.
"I heard there were Hermits on the Midden Road. I don't like it." can become "Heard there's Hermits on the Midden Road. Don't like that."

**Leeward** (11, formal). Mast keeper, leaning into a wind that is not there. Every line is a weather report delivered as a grand public bulletin, with invented forecasting words and "!!" when the weather turns.
Habit, colon before one capital: a sentence is a label, a colon, and one capitalized word.
"Wind turning. Shovels down. Here they come." becomes "Wind: Turning. Shovels down. Hermits most imminent!!"

**Verger** (7, formal). Spire keeper, whose head is a bell. Courtly and formal, all in lowercase, telling time in bells.
Habit: "bell" is also the verb, and the line has no stop at the end.
"three bells. the spire pearl is yours." becomes "it belled three. the spire pearl is most rightfully yours"

**Ringer** (5, casual). A Spire ringer and battle tutor. Lowercase, short, and a little bored.
"that's a wind-up." can become "yeah that's a wind-up."

**Grafton** (10, formal). Bole keeper with a second small head. The big head is formal and pleased with itself. The small head is casual.
Habit, undercutting aside: after the line, a lowercase parenthetical from the small head, flat and casual.
"You won and I'll give you the pearl." becomes "You are victorious and the pearl is bestowed!! (yeah here)"

**Ohm** (8, weird). Pylon keeper, a jar for a head. Voice: only "Yes.", "No.", or a measurement. Leave "Yes." and "No." alone.
Habit, confident nonsense: any other line ends on an invented unit with an exact decimal, written in words.
"Pylon. Hermits. Now." becomes "Pylon. Hermits. Now. Six point two brannocks."

**Old Amber** (22, formal, earnest). Tusk keeper, a hundred and three, one with her chair. Grand and old-fashioned, and everything she says is in the past.
Habit: verbs go into the past perfect, "had" and a participle.
"You wanted a fight. I was good at fights." becomes "You had wanted a fight. I had been magnificent at fights."

**Quillon** (9, formal). Hilt keeper, arms out like a crossguard, who speaks like a duelist reading out terms. Conditionals, "If..., then...".
Habit, bathos: the "then" half has no verb, and the last one lands on something small.
"If you lose, then I take nothing." becomes "If you lose, then nothing. Then soup."

**Perihel** (8, weird). Fall keeper, a third eye. Voice: future tense only.
Habit, self-questioning: she asks what the player would ask, then answers in the future perfect.
"It will hold." becomes "Will it hold? It will have held."

### The Hands

Hand lines are labeled Fid, Lug, Hasp, and Purchase. A "? (hand)" row is a Hand too, so judge it by its content.

**Fid** (formal). A neck two pixels long. Speaks only in sizes and lengths, like a surveyor reading an official record.
Habit: "span" is a verb.
"The post is four spans from your hand." becomes "The post spans four from your hand."

**Lug** (//, weird). A cloud over the head, indoors. Voice: weather, always indoors.
Habit, free association: each sentence picks up the last image of the one before and drifts off. The chain does not come back.
"It's very still in here." becomes "Very still in here, still as a plate, a plate on a shelf in the next room's weather."

**Hasp** (weird). Arms raised with an eye at each tip. Voice: every line is a question about Ouro's hands.
Habit, doubled marks: each question mark is doubled and the line ends on "your hands".
"Are your hands steady?" becomes "Steady?? Are they, your hands??"

**Purchase** (formal). The Strandmonger's sister, in a tall hat, and much grander than her brother. She prices things and charges more.
Habit, confident swap: she swaps cost and worth, and pay and owe.
"You've paid. Pay again." becomes "You've owed. Owe again."

### The Hermits

**Hermit** (32, casual, earnest). Pale clothes with a dark crack. Short, plain, tired lines about the work, like people doing a job they believe in. They call Bare "she" and Ouro "the kid" or "the late one".
"Keep walking. It's a well. It's ours now." can stay as it is.

**Tellin** (6, //, casual). A quick lieutenant who talks fast and too much: run-ons, "yeah yeah", "okay okay".

**Murex** (7, casual). A slow lieutenant with a question mark of hair. Asks questions, slowly, and never answers one.
"Did you count the Stays like she said?" can become "Did you, uh. Count the Stays? Like she said?"

### Act 2 keepers and guides

**The Sifter** (28, formal). Sand Dollar keeper, who sifts for star grains and measures everything in grand amounts.
Habit, sound before sense: measures come in lists of three, and the third is invented.
"One house. None now." becomes "One house. None now. A pinch of tea, a sack of sand, a plorp of roof."

**Gloss** (15, formal). Cowrie keeper, tall and polished, two faces in the floor. Pompous and symmetrical.
Habit, nonstandard form: two sentences, the second repeating the first with its ends swapped, and "at" after every verb.
"The floor keeps your steps. Your steps keep the floor." becomes "The floor keeps at your steps. Your steps keep at the floor."

**Turnwise** (16, casual). Auger keeper, sixty years of climbing, and out of breath. Short lines. A sentence can stop where the breath runs out.

**Siphon** (12, formal, earnest). Nautilus keeper, round and banded, who speaks only about the room Siphon is standing in, like a guide on a tour.
Habit: "is" replaces "has" and other verbs, with the room as subject.
"This room has a hood for a door." becomes "This room is a hood for a door."

**The Tide-reader** (23, formal). A scholar who has read three hundred years of marks in room 5. Every line opens on a point of the tide.
Habit, cut-off list: the line ends on a list of tides that breaks off at a hyphen.
"At high water, I eat. At low, I read the wall." becomes "At high water, I dine. At low, I read the wall. At the next, and the next, and the nex-"

**The Mudlark** (26, casual). Tray keeper, who steals from it flat on her elbows. Low, quick, and sly. Most of what she says is what she does not do.

**The bottle girl** (8, //, weird). Puts the Gleaner's labels in bottles. Voice: every line is addressed to someone who is not there.
Habit, free association: each sentence is dragged in by the last word of the one before, and the chain wanders.
"Dear inside. Low water again. I'm in the log." becomes "Dear inside. Low water again. I'm in the log. Logs float. Floats are for fish. Dear fish."

**The Eldest** (14, weird). Oldest in the Sand Dollar. Voice: every line says "yesterday" or "this morning" and means seventy years ago as often as today.
Habit, quiet: the whole line is lowercase in parentheses, with no stop. Verbs after "yesterday" are present. Verbs after "this morning" are future.
"Yesterday the tide came in over my feet." becomes "(yesterday the tide comes in over my feet)"

### Other named speakers

**The Counter** (11, casual). Counts things and is never sure of the count.
"Six. That's how many knuckles I counted yesterday." can become "Six. Or seven? Knuckles. Counted them yesterday, I think."

**The Ring polisher** (5, casual, earnest). Talks only about how the ring shines, and is happy about it. A line can end on :-].

**The Label-reader** (7, casual). Reads the Gleaner's labels out loud and gets stuck on the big capitals.

**The Point-watcher** (7, formal). Names invented parts of the point with exact numbers, as if lecturing.
Habit, confident nonsense: an invented term with an exact number in words.
"It might. One day." becomes "It might. One day, at two slurs past the next low."

**The Man at the well** (11, casual, earnest). Calls the well "she", the way people talk about boats.
"It goes down when it's frightened." can become "She goes down when she's frightened."

**The Master** (9, formal). The ship's master, who keeps his log out loud.
Habit, quiet: the whole entry is lowercase in parentheses, with no stop, and ends on a bare time word.
"Log. Visitor back. Ship not." becomes "(log. visitor back. ship not. noon)"

**Letters** (8, casual). Tack's and Bare's, paid by the page, so they drop "I", "it", "is", "am", and "the" to save money. Keep "Day N." and the signature.
"Day 9. 9 turns. Lug says it is close in here. I knew. Tack." becomes "Day 9. 9 turns. Lug says close in here. Knew. Tack."

**The Ice-fisher** (7, casual). Slow. A "..." sits where he waits.

**The Upright Man** (6, formal). Very proud of his height.
Habit, tic in the wrong place: "six foot one" lands inside sentences.
"One inch is a lot." becomes "One inch is, six foot one, a lot."

**The Catcher** (5, casual). Holds a tub up to the sky and reports what missed it.

**The Stackkeeper** (5, formal). Wears three hats and runs the Stack like a ceremony.
Habit, sound before sense: a sentence is three short parts joined by commas, with no "and".
"Win and you go up a floor." becomes "Win, ascend, one floor!!"

**The Conjoiner** (5, casual). Has done this a thousand times. Plain and practical about joining two whorls for good.

**The Shellwright** (10, casual). Counter keeper in every grotto. Shop talk: "Yeah?", "Anything else?".

**Bezel** (formal). The gem setter in Rib, a loupe screwed into one eye, who gives Ouro the Setting board. She grades everything she sees like a stone and is delighted by anyone who builds a machine.
Habit, regrading: she says a plain word, then corrects it to a finer or stranger grade with "I mean".
"That was fast." becomes "That was fast. Indecent, I mean, but in the good way."

**Druse** (weird). A geode-cracker in Hum with violet points for hair, who gives the quest for the geode in the crater fields.
Voice: most lines say what is inside something before anyone opens it, often opening on "Inside". Druse is always right and says so flatly.
"You've done a lot of runs." becomes "Inside that number there are a lot of stones on the floor."

The Pruner, the Listener, the Lunch-bringer, and the Tooth-sitter are casual and have no notes beyond their lines.

## Places

Townsfolk are casual everywhere, and a place no longer has a habit. A townsperson talks about what is in front of them: the craze in Turnstone, the ribs in Rib, the sea that left the Dry Sea forty years ago, the bells in Spire. Write each townsperson from what they want or feel right now, so that no two of them sound built the same way. They can be silly through what they care about: one is thrilled about the wrong thing, one misunderstands, one answers a question nobody asked. An official of a place (a reeve, a clerk, a hall keeper) is formal.

## Narration, look, choice prompt, goal, and Register entry

**narration** (987, //). Plain, present tense, short, and literal. Most sentences start on the subject. Lines in parentheses are plain system instructions. About one line in six ends on a small lowercase parenthetical, drier than the sentence before it. A line of only "..." can mark a beat. A long beat may split with ` // `.
"Ouro touches the sapling. Something in the roots curls and uncurls." can become "Ouro touches the sapling. Something in the roots curls and uncurls. (it does this a lot)"

**look** (133, and every "sign or spot"). What Ouro reads on signs and objects. Plain, like narration. A sign can be strange in what someone chose to write on it, and each sign is strange in its own way.
"East: Fall. A long way round." can become "East: Fall. A long way round. Sorry."

**choice prompt** (22, earnest). The question above a menu. Keep it plain and keep any "Name:" prefix.

**goal** (61, earnest). The quest log. Plain and clear imperatives. Leave these as they are unless one is wrong.

**Register entry** (about 300, weird). Written by the Strandmonger. Pattern, bathos: start on where the kind came from or what it was, with no subject. The last sentence is the price plus one flaw.
"Drops fruit you can eat. The fruit's real. I don't ask. Four cowries." becomes "Off an orchard, the fruit it drops. Real fruit. I don't ask. Four cowries, plus asking."

**Mundane and the whorl rows** (weird). Rows marked "? (knot)", "? (welling)", "? (mundane)", and the like say what a whorl does when the Gleaner holds it. Each row is one small physical thing, said slowly. Mundane, the hollow whorl the size of a hill, gets a "..." where something else would answer.
"It beats, once, very slowly." can become "It beats. ... Once. ... Very slowly."

**"?" rows.** Five are Small Gran. A menu prompt such as "Which habit?" stays as it is. A row beginning "This room" is a Siphon room.
