# Style guide for game text

This guide comes from the user's own examples in `Writing Examples.txt` at the project root. Read that file first. These patterns describe what makes the examples work. Use them as a toolbox that each speaker draws from according to `cast.md`, and never pile them all into one line. Do not copy the examples' names or lines (Spoza, Overknower, Vagabond, pilgrim, and the rest belong to the user's other work).

## Three registers come first

Every speaker belongs to one of three registers. Contrast between them is what makes a scene work, so assign the register before reaching for any pattern below. The user's own examples of the contrast:

> "SALUTATIONS young gentlegirl. We need food!! now!!!"
> "Uh yeah. There's a market."
> "Thanks :-]"

> "This foulish brutish evilish badman type guy is messing with us I think!"
> "Really?"
> "Yeah man."
> "I'm not an evillish badman :-("
> "...Yeah you kinda are."
> "Yeah I agree."
> ":-("
> "Frowning doesn't make it untrue."
> "Let's kill him."

1. **Casual.** Most side characters: townsfolk, trainers, kids, Hermits, shop staff. They talk the way people talk: short, loose, a bit flat, unimpressed. "Uh", "yeah", "kinda", "guy", "I think", trailing reactions, one-word answers ("Really?"). Contractions everywhere. Emoticons such as :-] and :-( are allowed now and then for the softest or saddest casual speakers. A casual speaker has no verbal habit and no strangeness of their own. Their flatness next to a weird or formal line is the joke.
2. **Formal.** A few characters speak grandly and get it slightly wrong: invented formal words ("gentlegirl", "evilish"), stacked adjectives, capital greetings, double and triple exclamation marks when they get excited. Keepers, officials, old families, and anyone who thinks they are important.
3. **Weird.** The characters with quirks from the patterns below: tics, loud capitals, lowercase whispers, confident nonsense, free association, glitches. Keep this register to the characters `cast.md` marks as weird.

Narration is its own voice: plain, present tense, with the occasional dry aside. Choices are the player's own words and stay plain and casual.

Narration calls Ouro "Ouro" and usually avoids pronouns. Where repeating the name gets clunky, use they and them for Ouro ("Their feet come up off the sand."). Never he or she. Gran's "the child... it" is her own voice and stays.

Violence, threat, and bad news can come out blunt and casual ("Let's kill him."). Underplaying a big moment is part of the contrast.

These quotes show the contrast. Do not reuse their exact words ("gentlegirl", "evilish badman", "SALUTATIONS") in the game. Invent the game's own.

## Every line its own shape

The user, on the current text: "there's a bit to much 'this is the x y and z and y. it does a because nobody ever asked.' those are just gonna stack up as a pattern and get tired quick. most dialogue should read as unique, and just be weird silly".

- The flat fact followed by a dry afterthought ("It sings. Flat, mostly.") is used up. Do not write it, and do not add a short tail sentence to an old line.
- No two lines in a scene share a construction, and no pattern from the list below repeats often enough to be noticed.
- Write each line from what the speaker wants or feels right now. Weird and silly comes from people: getting excited about the wrong thing, misunderstanding, going off on a tangent, answering a question nobody asked, overreacting, underreacting, or saying something plainly absurd as if it were normal.

## What the examples do

1. **Sound before sense.** Lists that run on rhythm and rhyme, with invented words slipped in among real ones: a list of five foods where two do not exist. Read the line aloud. If it is flat, it is wrong.
2. **Verbal tics that repeat.** "a sort of", "indeed", "anyways", "yes?", "Well?". A tic belongs to one speaker and comes back every time they talk. A tic can arrive in a grammatical place it should not: "There is a sort of: Wire."
3. **Broken punctuation on purpose.** Doubled marks (!! and ??). A capital interjection in mid-line followed by a lowercase restart ("Your shell. OH. how it blooms."). A list cut off with a hyphen ("biting and- there is nothing left"). A colon before one capitalized word. A whole line that is only "...". A loud rant with no final punctuation.
4. **Loud and quiet registers.** One voice speaks in capitals: breathless, run-on, piling "AND" on "AND", asking question after question. Another voice comes in lowercase inside parentheses, calm and far away, sometimes in rhyme with the word order bent ("left alone the flowers born").
5. **The aside that undercuts.** A narrating voice comments on itself in parentheses: "(you have bigger things to worry about)", "(like they always say)". The aside is drier and smaller than the sentence it follows.
6. **Bathos.** A cosmic sentence lands on something small: grand recipes followed by quiche, an apocalypse followed by "TURN THE LIGHTS OFF", a grand quest answered with "oh damn."
7. **Free association.** One image drags the next one in by sound or sense: copper to penny to "the penny has lost all value", heartache to kneeache. The chain wanders and does not come back.
8. **Glitches and errors that feel human.** A word repeated by accident ("HEY CAN YOU WHISTLE? HEY CAN YOU WHISTLE?"). Run-together words ("wellworthit"). Nonstandard forms ("anyways", "a dice", "goo'ed"). A word swapped for its near-opposite with full confidence ("never met informally"). Use these rarely and give each one to a speaker who would make it.
9. **Confident nonsense.** Invented technical words delivered as fact, with absurd exact numbers. The speaker never explains and never doubts.
10. **Self-questioning.** "THE YEAR? 1977 I THINK." A speaker asks themselves a question and answers it badly.
11. **Earnestness.** Not everything is strange or ironic. A cheerful helper is simply cheerful, a farewell letter is warm and a little formal, and a quest-giver means every grand word. The sincere voices make the strange ones land.
12. **Silence.** A line of "..." is a beat. Use it where a character does not answer.

## How it fits this game

- Every line still has to fit a text box: 100 characters at most. A long rant becomes several boxes. Split a line into more boxes with ` // ` between the parts, and keep every part under 100 characters. Use this sparingly, mostly for the loud voices.
- Keep what a line tells the player. A puzzle hint, a direction, a price, a number, or a rule must still be clear after the strangeness is added. Put the strangeness around the fact rather than inside it.
- Keep the in-world words from `cast.md` exactly.
- The validator still bans em dashes, en dashes, semicolons, and a short list of words: whisper, echo, shimmer, tapestry, testament, journey, destiny, ancient, "something shifted", "a part of me", "I guess", "Look,", seemed, almost, and "after a moment". Find another way to say those.
- The world is shells, beaches, tides, and falling stars. Avoid flesh and gore imagery: the user moved the game away from skin because it read as gross. A soft thing in a hard coat is fine.
- Narration stays present tense. It may use fragments, odd starts, and the undercutting aside, but it never explains what something means.
